# Capacity, leases and custody

## What this area is

`internal/alloc` is the allocator: the capacity ledger, the lease state machine (`validTransitions`), placement, floors, the compute barrier, quarantine, force operations, enrollment and revocation records. `internal/server` runs one listener per tier against GitHub's scale-set API and consumes escrow; `internal/nodeplane` dispatches commands to hosts over the wire and `internal/node` executes them, holding custody of what it cannot account for. The one sentence everything below serves: billet must never promise a machine it does not have, and capacity is handed back only on proof that the compute is gone.

## Rules

**Capacity is bought when a runner starts, and it is a vector.** Each tier is its own scale set with its own `maxCapacity`, and every tier advertises at once (`steadyAdvertisement`: committed work raised to `Allocator.AdvertisedCeiling`, which reads configuration only, so the number does not flicker with live headroom). GitHub.com assigns jobs directly (`JobAssigned`, request id 0, no offer, no `AcquireJobs`) and only to a scale set advertising capacity, and there is no way to decline an assignment; so the advertisement is permission to be assigned, not a promise of a machine. The advertisements together exceed the fleet by design, and an assignment that finds no room waits at GitHub. `Allocator.Escrow` reserves against the global ledger first (vCPU, memory, per-tier concurrency, macOS licence slots), and the headroom check and the insert are one transaction: measured, moving the check outside produced 28 grants against a ceiling of 4. `TestConcurrentReservationsNeverOvercommit` is the guard. Every reservation names a machine (`target_node` at escrow, `node` at bind) and capacity is charged on whichever is set, or a tier could escrow against the same host repeatedly before its first launch. Nothing runs without a lease: the invariant is enforced at the purchase, never at the advertisement.

**Nothing is reserved for an idle tier, and there is no rotation (#140).** v0.10.0 escrowed one slot per tier to advertise and wasted the catalogue's capacity (#116); v0.11.0 rotated one shared discovery turn across tiers, which on GitHub.com left each idle tier assignable for about one poll in eighteen minutes, and a job queued against an idle tier went unassigned for hours. Both are gone. Capacity is bought at three points and nowhere else: per offer in `handle` (`refillEscrowTo(targetCapacityFor(len(Available)))`, the classic `JobAvailable` path), per pool launch in `reconcilePool` (`backPoolSlot`, one lease at a time, launched before the next is bought), and per unpromised request-id assignment (`backAssignment`). `reconcilePool` sizes the pool to `TotalAssignedJobs`. Escrow bought and not used is released after the message against `targetCapacity`/`messageCapacityTarget`, never beneath the advertisement, because the advertisement is no longer backed. A seal or a drain buys nothing: `refuseIfSealed` fails closed on an unreadable admission read, and `refillEscrowUngated` treats it as "buy nothing", so an assignment in flight when a seal lands waits until it lifts (the host-upgrade fence relies on the seal being absolute). A more frequent `Escrow` error still stops the listener, as it always did. Ordering between tiers waiting on the same returning room is `admissionQueue` (`internal/server/admissionorder.go`), one per control plane, shared by every listener through `listenerOpts` and set from `server.admission_order` (`config.AdmissionOrder`, `fair` by default). IT GATES PURCHASES AND NEVER ADVERTISEMENTS, which is the whole lesson of #140: v0.11.0 rotated which tier could advertise, and a tier that is not advertising is never assigned the work the scheduler would have scheduled. Under `fair` the longest-waiting tier holds freed room until its shape fits (head-of-line blocking on purpose, and deadlock-free because there is one winner chosen by a wait it cannot extend); it is bounded by the longest running job ONLY WHILE THE WAITER'S LISTENER IS THERE TO BUY, which on 2026-09-23 it was not: the longest waiter's listener sat on a dead broker connection for 18 minutes, could neither buy nor clear its wait, and every tier sharing its host declined every assignment with 78 of 136 vCPU free. So a waiter is dated by its last admission progress (`waits` at every refused reconciliation, `launchBegins` and `launchEnds` at each end of every launch, never a mere successful exchange, because a held message is redelivered every poll while reconciliation is skipped). A launch still in flight is NOT progress. Until 2026-09-25 it was, and a waiter launching eight slots one at a time into a node whose command queue took four to five minutes a launch held every tier for twenty minutes. The two marks sit inside `Listener.launch`, so the pool and direct-assignment paths both carry them. Then `mayBuy` lets another tier past a waiter with no progress for `WaiterAllowance` (three minutes, two of the longest measured long polls, a policy choice) while the waiter keeps its `since` and takes the line back the moment it progresses; the transitions are logged once each and only when a buyer really got ahead (`letPast`), and `billet status` prints NO ADMISSION PROGRESS PUBLISHED from the published `waiting_progress`: an observation rather than the queue's decision, because a stalled listener cannot publish its own stall and the queue can see a launch mark before the next publication. This weakens fairness, never capacity: the queue releases, destroys and promises nothing (`admissionliveness_test.go`); under `fill` anything that fits buys. A tier is recorded as waiting by `reconcilePool` when it could not buy for demand it has, and released when its demand is met OR when GitHub's count says the work is gone — a record that outlived its demand would hold the fleet behind nothing, which is worse than the starvation it prevents. The first refusal dates the wait, ties break by label, and a nil queue (a standalone listener) admits everything. `refillEscrow` has no production caller and stages escrow in tests. EVERY PURCHASE OF A LEASE ASKS IT, not only the pool path: `backAssignment` and the offer path gate on `mayBuy` too, because on GitHub.com the work arrives as direct assignments and a stream of small ones took every fragment of room a large tier was accumulating (#157). What it does not gate is `alloc.Resize`, the remote fallback growing an EXISTING lease to the shape the cloud sold: that is re-authorised against the ceiling and every floor, the order admitted the lease already, and the resize is asked for by the node (the plane's resize route, or the in-process runtime) and the queue is handed only to listeners, so nothing on that path holds it (a wiring fact, not a layering one: `nodeplane` imports `server`) — so a fallback to a larger shape can take pot a waiter was accumulating. A TIER HOLDS THE LINE ONLY WHERE ROOM COULD REACH IT: `waits` is recorded only when `alloc.AdmissionView` says the tier could grow once current work ends, so a tier at its own cap, pinned to a host that is gone, or with a shape no live host fits waits without freezing the fleet. `CanGrow` errs towards true where the model is uncertain, because a tier wrongly excluded is starved while one wrongly included only waits its turn, and it is three questions in order. The modelled total is compared with what the tier holds ON THE MODELLED FLEET (`countOpenPerTier`, where a lease stranded on a departed host counts for nothing, as it does for a floor). Then its own `max_concurrent` is compared with EVERY open lease (`countOpenByTier`), live host or not, because that is how the cap is enforced: counted on the live fleet alone, a tier whose whole cap sits on a departed host is told it can grow and holds the order behind a purchase the allocator refuses. Anything the model refuses is re-asked as `headroom`, the room the allocator would grant this instant. The model charges floors through `reserveFloors`, the same function the allocator charges them with, so only what is OUTSTANDING is held; holding every configured floor in full re-placed one already backed elsewhere and excluded the tier whose only host the model then spent. `Nodes` are the hosts an empty fleet could place the tier's SHAPE on, not every host the policy would consider, or a waiter would block tiers a host it cannot use can really serve. A waiter blocks a tier when they share such a host OR when `CeilingShared` holds for both: that is a fact about the DEPLOYMENT, the ceiling being smaller than what the live hosts could hold ONCE WHAT IS CHARGED OFF THE FLEET IS PAID (`ceilingSharedOver`, over `usage` minus `usageByNode` on the live hosts, because a lease stranded on a departed host holds the ceiling where no live host can use it), and not about a tier — asking instead whether the ceiling cut one tier's own total said no for an 8 vCPU tier on one host and a 4 vCPU tier on another under an 8 vCPU ceiling, which fit under it separately and not together, and the small one starved the large one on a host it never touched. A waiter's hosts are refreshed at every refusal while its PLACE is dated from the first, and an unknown host set competes with everyone. The view is read AFTER the purchases when the record is written, because a tier that reached its cap inside the loop could still grow when the loop began; a read that fails keeps the tier eligible with an unknown host set, which is not a way past the order but competes with every waiter.

**A target's share is a narrower deployment ceiling (#192).** `Limits.Shares` (from `config.Config.TargetShares`, passed at every `alloc.Limits` the CLI builds) caps what one target's tiers hold together. `readShareUsage` sums `UsageByTier` over the tiers the catalogue gives each target; a lease whose tier left the catalogue is charged against EVERY share (its target cannot be told, and forgetting it sells its room twice). `reserveFloors` holds each target's outstanding floors only up to that target's remaining share, at the charged shape (the placer's deployment room is set to the share budget), and returns what it held per target, so `headroomWithPlacer` and `Resize` subtract a sibling's floor from the share and an impossible remote floor cannot take the deployment from other targets. `headroomWithPlacer` narrows the placer's deployment room to the share; `Resize` authorises as if the lease's current shape had been returned; the admission model bounds a tier by the whole share less its siblings' floors. `TierAdmission.TargetShared` makes one target's share a pot like `CeilingShared` (siblings pinned to different hosts compete), and `ShareBound` says the share, measured at the cheapest charged shape rather than the request, is what stops the tier now. `competes(buyer, waiter)` is directional: only a share-bound WAITER stops holding another target's buyers, never a buyer's own full share, and only when both targets are known. The flag is the waiter's last view, so it can be one poll stale in the direction that favours the other target.

**Status separates ownership from phase.** Migration 50 carries dated listener observations with exact discovery and pending lease IDs, the last sent advertisement and the last completed exchange. `CapacityReport` joins that observation to still-open ledger rows, reports unclassified capacity as unknown, and separates pending, launching, running, cleanup, configured floors and additional headroom. A failed exchange is ambiguous; neither headroom nor a missing report means zero advertised capacity. These records decide no scheduling or release.

**A commitment made to a remote service cannot be revoked by a local timer.** `AcquireJobs` is one-way; there is no decline. Ageing out a stale `acquiring` promise was written, reviewed and reverted: it hands nothing back to GitHub, it only makes billet forget it owes a runner, and the assignment arrives with nothing behind it. A second attempt released on GitHub's own statistics and was the same bug, because a freshness check on your own record is not a causal fence on somebody else's snapshot. A stale promise is reported and kept; invalidating the session is what makes GitHub redeliver.

**The lease state machine is written down and terminal phases have no successors.** `capacity → assigned → launching → online → busy`, with `custody`, `teardown` and `quarantine` as holding states and `done`/`failed` terminal; `quarantine` exits only to a terminal. `requiresPlacement` (launching, online, busy) refuses to enter a running phase without a bound, still-legal placement, re-checked against policy in force then; `hadCompute` decides whether an expired lease is failed outright or quarantined. A row whose placement facts are unverifiable fails closed, and `Reap` and `Release` deliberately skip those checks so it can always be cleaned up.

**A remote lease is charged for the shape, not the tier request.** A node registers its ordered shapes; placement picks the first that fits and stores both the requested resources and the charged shape. A fallback is a new purchase decision authorised atomically against node and deployment ceilings before the request is sent. The predicate is `RunsOnHost`, not `== ec2`; a third remote backend added without touching that line would be charged the smaller tier request while buying the shape. The shape's price (`leases.price_micros_per_hour`) and the placed host's site are written on the lease when the shape is charged, at escrow and again on a resize, and `archive` copies them with the provider, the shape and the charged vector onto `job_history` (migration 49); the price is never re-read from the node's catalogue, because `sameEC2PlacementShapes` ignores price and a node may re-register with new rates while the lease is open. `ListExpiredLeases` selects `chosen_provider` because the reaper archives from that projection; before it did, every reaped lease was archived as having run on nothing.

**The epoch is a fence and a reclaim bumps it.** Every lease write presents its epoch and a stale one is refused with `ErrFenced`, or a holder declared dead keeps writing to a lease somebody else owns. A registration bumps the node's epoch; the process's incarnation is separate (a restart re-registers with a new epoch and the same process registers again after every control-plane restart), which is why `leases.holder_incarnation` records the incarnation of the process holding compute and never the epoch.

**A heartbeat pass's budget starts after it holds the mutex.** Created at the tick, the deadline is spent waiting for billet's own lock, the pass reaches the allocator already expired, and `renew` reads its own silence as the allocator's; past the TTL that is `renewalStale`, which tears down a healthy job. Measured: a 700ms stall against a 300ms TTL reproduced a CI failure exactly. `Listener.heartbeatLock` is the seam a test stands in. Heartbeats are never bounded by the poll (the first real poll ran 88 seconds against a 90-second TTL), the cadence derives from the allocator's actual TTL rather than `DefaultLeaseTTL` (measured at six times the budget otherwise), and `assign` holds the mutex across the ledger write. A transient heartbeat error keeps the lease; only `ErrFenced` and `ErrLeaseNotFound` mean it is somebody else's.

**Exactly one party renews a lease, and every defect here was a moment when that count was zero.** Three handovers: the node renews from the moment it commits to launching, because the plane gives up at the command timeout while the provider is still working; the plane records a tombstone for every launch it abandons so a late success is told "this lease is yours" rather than filed unrenewed; and `settle` drains the result channel under the plane mutex so a result racing the timeout is answered exactly. A listener holds no compute, so on `ErrHolderUnavailable` it parks the obligation without the lease (`parkUnreachable`); renewing a lease it could not reach the holder of built a loop with no exit, measured against real CodeBuild.

**Compute billet cannot account for is a named state, never resold.** `internal/node/custody.go` holds adopted compute (survived a restart; the runner may finish the job) and discarded compute (an ambiguous launch whose cleanup was not confirmed). Both renew the lease; both release only when the compute is proved gone. A negative observation is not automatically causal: a `docker ps` right after a lost `docker run` can overtake the daemon, so an uncausal absence waits through `strayGrace`; a remote backend waits the full grace every time. "Could not verify" is not "safe to destroy"; only `ErrLeaseNotFound` proves a lease gone. A cleanup obligation is owed to the compute, not the lease: losing the lease changes who owns the capacity, not whether the container runs.

**`Recover` runs at every registration, not only at start, so what it finds includes this process's own jobs (#227).** `Holding()` counts `running` as well as custody and `launching`, and a drain ends only when all three are empty. So an instance is in `running` or in custody, never both: every custody installer drops the matching `running` entry (`forgetRunningLocked`), because custody's `finish` removes only its own entry. When recovery or the sweep proves an orphan destroyed, `forgetRunning` drops it too. An unconfirmed teardown keeps its entry, because the compute may still be there. Measured 2026-09-25: a node the control plane had forgotten re-registered, destroyed eleven finished jobs' VMs as orphans, kept all eleven in `running`, and its drain ninety minutes later waited six and a half hours on compute that was gone. A goroutine dump showed nothing in flight: `waitForHolding` was ticking `Tend` over an empty custody map, and the hour-old custody warning never fired.

**Quarantine holds capacity for compute nobody has accounted for.** A lease that had compute and stopped heartbeating is quarantined, still charged to its host, and leaves only on proof: the node's destroy report, an inventory that omits it after the grace, or an operator's `leases release --force`. `ResolveQuarantineFor` settles from a fresh observation; a first version settled a completion from the plane's cached inventory snapshot, which has no ordering against the quarantine and would free capacity under a running build. Every completion path that cannot reach its holder corrects a verdict something else recorded (`SettleCompletionOnTerminalLease`) and never settles one. A lease whose completion GitHub already delivered settles as `done`, not as a provisional failure.

**Time warns; it does not authorise a teardown.** Held compute has no bound by default (`DefaultMaxCustody = 0`) and warns hourly; billet imposes no job limit and GitHub does not requeue a job whose runner vanished after starting (checked against the vendored client's README: reassignment covers a job "not acquired by a runner in time" and nothing more). `server.drain_timeout` used to bound the shutdown wait and then destroy what was left; it is now the point where `warnDrainOverrun` starts repeating every fifteen minutes. `destroyAll` takes `includeRunning`; a shutdown passes false, and `billet force-destroy` is the only caller of true, asserted structurally by `TestOnlyTheForceOperationDestroysRunningWork` because nothing in the type system separates the two booleans. Force refuses unless an operator sealed admission, fixes its target set when the request is taken (a standing flag would be a timer wearing different clothes), and archives forced leases as `failed`.

**A listener's drain does not wait for an anonymous pool slot that never started a job (#220).** GitHub hands such a slot no job while its tier drains. Measured 2026-09-25: slots launched a minute before a host upgrade's drain sat idle for sixteen minutes with jobs queued for their tier. The drain waited on them until a second signal ended it, because the drain-time reconcile retires only surplus beyond `TotalAssignedJobs`, and the declined assignments kept that count above them. `idleMembersOnly` ends the drain when every entry left in `running` has no `runningJobs` identity (a runner launched for an assigned job is still waited for) and the ledger records it `PoolRunnerIdle` with no actual request. That must still hold after a further completed poll, because a Started for the slot can be in the next message. The slots are left registered and charged, exactly as the second signal leaves them, and the successor adopts them. A pool read that fails keeps the drain waiting.

**A drain is two barriers, and the second asks the machines.** `alloc.Quiescence` answers from the ledger and cannot see compute whose lease has already gone (an in-memory destroy obligation, a launch whose lease was reclaimed); both leave an instance carrying billet's name on the host's provider. So `billet drain --wait` writes a durable barrier request the control plane observes, and the plane queues `CommandInventory` behind each host's serial queue. What makes an answer evidence is the fence: `nodes.dispatch_generation` is bumped durably before a launch enters the queue, under the same hold of `Plane.mu` (`BumpDispatch`), and an observation is recorded only while that number and the epoch are unchanged. A proof is two observations spanning `computeAbsenceGrace` (`observed_at − empty_since`), never one observation and a clock: the first version compared `now` against `empty_since + grace` and a host that answered empty once and disconnected aged into proof. A host saying it holds compute beats a completed run and may block but never clear (`provenBy` is the single definition the report and the decommission read). `Plane.ArrivingForRegistration` discards the host's stored run before a registration is judged, on both wiring paths, before the body is decoded and before revocation is checked, because a permanently refused registration is still an authenticated host reporting live compute. A host below `VersionComputeBarrier` is reported as unprovable; `billet nodes decommission` bounds the expected set and its `--force` records the exclusion as durably unproven.

**A clean exit withdraws; silence still means nothing.** `stopGracefully` sends `POST /v1/nodes/{node}/withdraw` after its wait on every exit and never from a superseded process. `alloc.NodeWithdrawn` writes `live = 0` fenced on epoch and incarnation, releases nothing, marks no disruption and discards no barrier run; the plane writes the ledger first and drops the node from memory second. Before it existed a stopped host kept receiving placements for one `staleAfter` window, about four and a half minutes measured. The window cannot be shortened, because a crash and a partition are one observation.

**A restart waits for its own message session, and so does a failover.** GitHub answers a second session for a scale set whose first was abandoned with `409 Conflict` (`ErrSessionHeld`), measured against a real organization under the old holder's name and under another host's; `openSession` waits it out, because that is the one refusal that resolves by itself. The bracket below is the ORIGINAL owner's wait; a differently named successor was measured only as far as the refusal. Measured 2026-09-04 on eight runs: the abandoned session was still refused at 60 seconds every time and open at 91 on three of them and 92 on the other five (a bracket, not an expiry), and the successor was then handed the id the abandoned session had been holding, carrying the same one assigned job (the run that compared identities rather than counts found the same run id and the same stable workflow-job id). Nothing is lost in the window: GitHub queues a job for 24 hours.

**An inventory that shrinks is capacity that gets resold.** A provider's `List` must error rather than answer short, because reconciliation frees every lease absent from it. Ask what makes a row appear in the inventory, then what could make that stop being true while the compute keeps running (a chroot named after a resolved binary an operator can retarget, a teardown that removed the pid file it needed, an identity mismatch collapsed into "not running").

**billet attributes a lost job; it never re-runs one.** `leases.disruption` is a token from a closed set written in the transaction that observed the disruption (`NodeGone`, `ResolveQuarantineFor`, `MarkFailure`), never reconstructed at completion, because `ForgetEveryNode` sets `live = 0` fleet-wide at every start and a later verdict would blame every host. `job_history.result` is GitHub's word. `billet leases failures` shows both side by side and says the pairing is circumstantial. `disruptionGuard` keeps the first observation and refuses a lease that already carries a result. The token is decided by the exact reason and by `job_history.started_at` before the reason's text, so a node cannot suppress it for a job the ledger saw start; `force-released` is refused over the wire.

**A reserved fleet's claim is released by a proof.** Two nodes naming one CodeBuild fleet each register its whole capacity. Refusing a second claimant while the holder was `live` was wrong (liveness says nothing about remote compute), and while it was not decommissioned was wrong (`--force` is not a proof). What releases it is `decommission_proven = 1`, which the drain's compute barrier writes. Permission to hand over shared capacity comes from what is proved, never from what is broken.

## Measured facts

- 28 grants against a ceiling of 4 with the headroom check outside the transaction.
- First real long poll: about 88 seconds against a 90-second lease TTL.
- Cadence from `DefaultLeaseTTL` under a shorter configured TTL: advertised capacity climbed to six times the budget.
- 700ms mutex stall versus a 300ms TTL reproduces `Running() = 0, want 1` in `TestACancellationInsideTheLongPollStillDrains`.
- A live node destroys a stray 45ms after its lease goes terminal, which is why the compute-barrier e2e test stops the control plane first and sets the reaper tick to an hour.
- A stopped host kept receiving placements for about 4.5 minutes before the withdraw route existed.
- Docker's `--filter name=X` is a substring match: `billet-abc` returns `billet-abcdef`; `Find` compares exactly afterwards.
- The shutdown grace was 90 seconds against a 10-minute node command timeout, so ordinary slow destroys tripped it; a bound must be larger than the longest legitimate operation under it.
- A CodeBuild holder-gone reproduction found a restart re-adopting a running teardown as custody (refused on every tend) and a teardown outcome that lived only in the dead process's memory; both fixed, both in `internal/e2e/holdergone_test.go`.
