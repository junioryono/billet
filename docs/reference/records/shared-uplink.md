# The shared uplink

What a morning of failing fetches from the reference deployment's guests turned out to be, measured on 2026-09-11, and what billet changed because of it. The deployment is one EC2 control plane (`control-01`) and one bare-metal Firecracker node (`linux-01`) at a residential site, with billet's own CI running on that node's guests since PR #111.

## What failed

Between 08:30 and 09:00 UTC every CI job that reached the internet beyond GitHub failed, each with a different message: apt's `Connection failed [IP: 185.125.190.83 80]` against `archive.ubuntu.com`, then over HTTPS `Could not wait for server fd - select (11: Resource temporarily unavailable) [IP: 185.125.190.82 443]` (apt's own 30-second bound expiring on a stalled connection), a `TLS handshake timeout` from Docker Hub, and `read: connection reset by peer` from `proxy.golang.org`. In the same window a `curl` of a Trivy release from GitHub stalled for five minutes before its own timeout ended it (exit 28), so the stalls were not one vendor's. Two or three full CI runs were on the fleet at once through that window (08:38 to 09:05 UTC), each with jobs pulling Go modules, container images and packages. A probe run from a guest at 08:19 UTC, before that load, had been clean. The operator reported the same "clogging" from a laptop at the same site.

## What was measured

**The mirror outage, from two unrelated networks.** From the site (the laptop and a fleet guest, which share one residential fiber uplink) and from the control plane (AWS us-west-2), every one of the nine IPv4 addresses `archive.ubuntu.com` resolves to refused TCP port 80 for hours (`curl --max-time 6` returned no connection to any of them), while `deb.debian.org` and `example.com` answered on port 80 at once. Over 443 seven of the nine answered, two of them slowly (1.5 to 3.2 seconds to a first byte from the AWS side), and `185.125.190.82` and `.83` answered on neither port. The laptop's `curl http://archive.ubuntu.com/` succeeded only because it has native IPv6 and the AAAA answers were healthy; a guest has no IPv6 route and the control plane no IPv6 address, so both saw a mirror with no working address. A status aggregator recorded Canonical incidents for `archive.ubuntu.com` and `security.ubuntu.com` (and `lists.ubuntu.com` and Landscape) through the same hours. Nothing at the site caused this, and nothing at the site could have fixed it.

**Where a guest's traffic goes.** From a guest, `https://www.cloudflare.com/cdn-cgi/trace` reported `warp=off` and the site's public IPv4 address (the laptop's, by the same probe), `ifconfig.me` the same address, and `ping -M do -s 1472 1.1.1.1` succeeded, so the guest's egress is plain NAT through the node onto the site's residential gateway with a 1500-byte path MTU; the node's WARP connector carries only the Zero Trust ranges (its device profile is in Include mode) and none of the guest's traffic. Idle, the path was clean: 7 ms to `1.1.1.1`, DNS answers in 8 to 13 ms serially and a burst of 300 concurrent lookups through the bridge's dnsmasq at p95 6 ms with no failure, five TLS handshakes each to Docker Hub, `proxy.golang.org`, GitHub and the GitHub container CDN all under 0.2 seconds, 100 parallel HTTPS requests all answered under 0.7 seconds, and zero retransmits or SYN retries in the guest's TCP counters. One of five HTTPS attempts against `archive.ubuntu.com` stalled the full 15 seconds, on the Canonical address that answered nothing.

**What the two hosts at the site share.** The node and the laptop sit on one LAN behind one residential gateway (a fiber gateway at 1 Gbps, no WAN drops or errors in its counters, 25 days up), and every guest's connection is one entry in that gateway's NAT table beside the laptop's. Its users report (forum and community threads, not a vendor document) that this class of gateway caps its NAT table at 8192 sessions on older firmware and 32767 on newer, and drops new connections as it approaches the cap, which reads at a laptop as pages that stall while existing connections keep working. The session count is on the gateway's diagnostics page behind its access code, which this session did not have; whether the fleet reached the cap during the failing hour is therefore not measured, only consistent with every symptom the guests and the laptop showed during a full CI run and with none of them while the fleet was idle. The laptop's `networkQuality` measured idle latency of 15 ms and loaded latency of 103 ms (responsiveness "Medium") at a quiet moment.

**Two things the bridge's own configuration did.** The node's nftables input chain accepts only DHCP, DNS and the cache endpoint from a guest and drops everything else, so `ping 172.31.1.1` from a guest proves nothing about the gateway: a probe that treats that as reachability fails on a healthy host. And the bridge's dnsmasq ran with its defaults, 150 cached names and 150 concurrent forwarded queries, sized for a home router rather than for a fleet of guests asking for the same few hundred names thousands of times an hour.

**Lost queries, measured again on 2026-10-06.** Guest jobs failed intermittently for about two hours with connect timeouts (Node's `fetch` giving up with `AggregateError [ETIMEDOUT]` about three seconds after its first attempt, Terraform's `context deadline exceeded` against its registry) and a test's three-second DNS deadline expiring. The node's own probe of the same registries saw no failure in that period. Neither did the gateway's ping, and the bridge's dnsmasq logged nothing. An eleven-minute capture on the node's uplink during ordinary CI load counted 9 of 1304 outbound TCP handshakes needing a SYN retransmit (0.7%) and 2 of 392 queries from the bridge's dnsmasq to its upstream resolvers getting no answer (0.5%). dnsmasq sends a query to one upstream and does not retry it. A lost one therefore costs the guest its resolver's retry interval, five seconds for glibc, so at that rate a job making a hundred uncached lookups has roughly a two-in-five chance of one stall that long.

## What changed

- The guest image carries `apparmor` and `python3-apt`, which every CI job on the fleet had been installing from the archive (with the parser present, dockerd in a guest confines containers under `docker-default`, as a hosted runner does), and its apt configuration names two HTTPS mirrors (`archive.ubuntu.com`, then `mirrors.edge.kernel.org`) with a 30-second inactivity bound and three retries per fetch; CI's two install steps skip apt when the package's status is `installed`, and PR #114 gives CI's own apt calls the same two mirrors on a guest whose image predates this. A job on a rebuilt guest depends on the archive for nothing the image carries; for the rest, an install can succeed from the surviving repository when it indexes and serves the selected version, while a strict `apt-get update` still fails while either mirror is down. A guest on the older image is covered only by PR #114's steps. Measured on a guest the same day, with the first URI black-holed and a 5-second inactivity timeout: apt treats the two URIs as two repositories, fetches both index sets, fails a strict `apt-get update` (`Error-Mode=any`) in 13 seconds naming the dead mirror, passes a lenient one in 13 seconds, and installs a package from the second mirror in 4 seconds. The alternative, apt's `mirror+file:` method with a priority list, did not finish a strict update in nine minutes under the same black hole (the probe job's ten-minute budget ended it), so the two-source shape is the one shipped. Not measured: a connection that stalls after connecting rather than refusing to connect, which is the shape the failing job saw.
- The host role sizes the bridge's resolver for a compute host (`billet_guest_dns_cache_size: 10000`, `billet_guest_dns_forward_max: 1000`), so the fleet's repeated lookups are answered on the node and are not sessions on the uplink.
- The bridge's dnsmasq runs with `all-servers` (2026-10-06): every forwarded query goes to every upstream in `billet_guest_dns_servers` and the first answer wins, so one lost packet no longer stalls a guest's lookup for a resolver retry. With two upstreams it doubles the uplink's DNS packets, which the cache already keeps few.

- Every host the role converges keeps its own share of the line in check (`billet uplink shape`, `billet-uplink.service`, 2026-10-08). On the same site with a 500 Mbit/s line, the node moving 378 Mbit/s down and 180 up gave 2.5% loss and latency from 7 to 29 ms to the node and to a laptop beside it, felt there as lag in an online game; with CAKE holding the node to 400 Mbit/s each way, set by hand as the first measurement, 80 pings each from both machines lost none while the node still pulled 342. The shipped shaper needs no rate: it follows cake-autorate's approach (latency to reflectors and load decide each direction's rate) and learns the line by cutting a busy direction to below what was flowing when the queue rose.

- **The shaper that learns from latency alone did not protect the site (2026-10-14).** The node's shaper restarted onto a new release at 02:05 UTC, starting each direction at the interface's 1 Gbit/s as designed, and cut nothing for the next three hours. Across 10-13 it had cut only seven times. The node ran CI the whole time, with its jobs recording 10 to 17 GB received and up to 16 GB sent per ten minutes (a share of that to the node's own caches), and in a measured burst at 05:15 it sent 385 to 888 Mbit/s for half a minute, an Android build uploading 3 GB to Play. A laptop behind the same gateway (a BGW320-500, WAN counters showing 0 receive drops and 5 transmit drops) lost API connections through that window, and pinging the gateway itself from the laptop went from a 1.6 ms minimum to a 10.6 ms average with spikes to 128 ms, while the round trip to 1.1.1.1 averaged about 11 ms above its 7 ms floor. That is under the 15 ms the shaper needs in 3 of 6 rounds. CAKE was set to 400 Mbit/s each way by hand at about 05:20 UTC (the rate that measured clean on 10-08), and `billet uplink shape` gained `--max-up` and `--max-down`, rendered from `billet_uplink_max_up_mbit` and `billet_uplink_max_down_mbit`: a ceiling each direction starts at and never rises past, with the latency rule still cutting below it.

- **A lost probe was read as an unbounded delay, and loss was no signal of its own (2026-10-15, #500).** A Linux node on a home line (a 1 Gbit/s NIC, a 500/500 line behind a consumer gateway) logged cuts with `delay=2562047h47m16.854775807s`, which is `math.MaxInt64`: 8 of the 22 cuts that day. The shaper represented a reflector that did not answer within the round's 400 ms as an unbounded round trip and took the median, so a round in which two of three reflectors went unanswered read as a measured delay of 292 years. The cut it caused was the right direction by accident, logged as a measurement, and the futility rule then compared the next delay against it. The same blindness let a rate climb: a round in which no reflector said anything counted as no queue. Meanwhile loss below a majority of the reflectors moved nothing, so a gateway that drops rather than queues read as a clear line. On that line a 400 Mbit/s ceiling let the node pull about 410 Mbit/s down while a Wi-Fi laptop on the LAN saw its ping to 1.1.1.1 swing to 25-32 ms; at 150 it held about 9.5 ms. Since then an unanswered probe is a loss and never a delay, a round with no measurement neither cuts nor raises and is logged as such, and probe loss in at least 2 of 6 rounds from most of the reflectors cuts a busy direction to three quarters of what it moves (the reasoning and its sources are in `internal/uplink`'s package comment). The ceiling stays: the loss rule acts once the line is already dropping.

- **An automatic upgrade left the shaper on the old release (2026-10-16).** A rollout moved a Linux node from v0.16.5 to v0.16.6; `billet host-upgrade` stopped and started `billet-node.service` and replaced `/usr/bin/billet`, and never touched `billet-uplink.service`, whose process went on running the deleted old binary. Its own check for a replaced executable runs once a minute, and an operator restarted it first, 49 seconds after the swap. That stop's `billet uplink clear` printed "no record of shaping on this host; nothing to clear" while the qdiscs had been live a moment before: the shaper removes its own shaping and its record as it stops, said nothing about it, and the clear without a record looked at nothing before saying so. Now the transaction restarts a running, enabled shaper after a commit and after a rollback (a failure reported, never deciding the upgrade), the shaper logs what it removed as it stops, and a clear with no record inspects the interface and refuses to say "nothing to clear" over CAKE, an ingress qdisc or its `ifb-` device; a record it cannot read is an error rather than no record.

- **The gateway is asked, what was learned outlives a restart and a reboot, and a rate near the interface's speed is no signal (2026-10-16, #493).** Measured read-only on the reference node that day, with its shaper at a 150 Mbit/s ceiling each way: the shaper had cut 96 times in the previous 24 hours, most of them on loss (`lost=2/3`). The node's default route goes by `eno2np1` to the BGW320 at 192.168.1.254 (`/proc/net/route` prints it `FE01A8C0`, in the host's byte order). Idle, the gateway answered 60 of 60 pings at 2/s in 0.30 to 0.90 ms. Over ten minutes of CI load that held the node at about 150 Mbit/s up, and then about 150 down, it answered 1,167 of about 1,191 (2.0% unanswered, against 1.7% for 1.1.1.1), with a median of 0.56 ms, a 99th percentile of 1.11 ms and one answer of 15.9 ms, while 1.1.1.1 held a median of 6.9 ms: at that ceiling CAKE keeps the queue on the node, and the gateway never stalled. A stall like the laptop's on 10-14 was therefore not reproduced, because lifting the ceiling to provoke one would have disturbed the site. What changed: the shaper asks the default route's gateway each round against a baseline of its own, following the route when it moves, its rise past 15 ms confirmed by another high reading at most four rounds before it, with no quiet pair between, counts as the reflectors' delay does, a lone spike excepted, a confirmed pair with no reply read between covers at most one earlier round too, the nearest that counted a reflector, its first reading's included, and a quiet reading just after a stall clears nothing (only in a round that counted a reflector, and a cut is judged against the same source's delay at the cut, on two readings, and on the reflectors' loss only when the line was dropping at the cut), and its unanswered probes are never loss (at 2% with the line quiet, read as loss they would cut a busy host by chance). A cut that follows one proved to have left the queue no shorter is the deep one, 0.75 rather than as little as 0.99, since the futility latch stops cutting after it and a gentle cut may only have been too gentle for a queue that was the host's own. The rates CAKE is set to, and those in force every minute and at a stop, are kept in `/var/lib/billet-uplink/learned`, and the next run starts there, returned toward the ceiling by half for each hour it was down, so neither the restart every upgrade causes nor a reboot starts the line at the interface's speed (the reference node lost its NIC's link at 17:09 UTC on 2026-10-17 and was hard-reset, and a learned rate under `/run` would have died with it). Within one boot a record is matched to the interface by the kernel's index, which catches another device under the same name; across a reboot, which renumbers interfaces, by its name and, where both are known, its hardware address as at the last record, which a NIC swapped while the host was off does not share. It is installed with an fsync of the file and its directory, so the hard reset it is kept for leaves the last whole record. A record from a clock ahead of this one, as on a host without a hardware clock booted before its time is set, is refused and replaced: holding it would hold back every later record, and the configured ceiling bounds the line meanwhile. And a sustained rate near the interface's speed with no ceiling set is deliberately not a signal: the shaper cannot tell a line as fast as the interface, where such a rule would throttle a healthy host for good, from a slower one, where a gateway that drops shows as the reflectors' loss; the ceiling stays the operator's lever, and 150 Mbit/s is what protects this site.

- **The line is found and kept, not configured (2026-10-17).** The reference node still needed `billet_uplink_max_up_mbit` and `billet_uplink_max_down_mbit` at 150 set by hand, and a ceiling is a number every site would have to measure for itself. Three things made it necessary. A run with nothing learned started each direction at the interface's speed, unshaped until its first cut. After every cut the rate climbed 4% each half second straight back past the line it had just found, so a gateway that drops rather than queues, which the shaper sees only once the loss shows, was overrun again within seconds of every cut. And a learned rate returned toward the interface's speed by half for each hour the shaper was down, so a host off for a day came back at a gigabit. Measured read-only that day, with the shaper stopped for 45 seconds: the node received 301 Mbit/s over 20 seconds while its round trip to 1.1.1.1 averaged 6.9 ms with a 10.2 ms maximum and 0.4% loss, and a Mac on the same LAN read 1.1.1.1 at a median of 9.5 ms, a 95th percentile of 10.9 and a 99th of 14.1 with 0.3% loss, the same as with the 150 ceiling in force; the upload half did not run, and a heavier probe to find where the line starts dropping was not run, because it would have disturbed the work on the node. What changed: each direction keeps where the line was last found full, what it was moving at its last cut, and climbs back at 4% a step only to nine tenths of that; from there it rises 2% at most every ten seconds (two percent being the least rise the shaper applies, so the rate it judges what flows against is always the one CAKE holds), and near the line, or before a cut has found one, only while it moves nine tenths of its rate, so a rate the line itself stops short of climbs no further. What a direction moved is taken as its line only when it was moving at least half its rate and at least a twentieth of what the other direction moved: both directions are cut on one verdict, and an upload carrying a download's acknowledgements, 12 Mbit/s beside 510, would otherwise have been held near 12 for many minutes after every busy download. Half its rate alone was not enough, because the first such cut leaves the upload at a rate its acknowledgements fill, and the next download's cut then took them as its line; acknowledgements are about 2 to 3% of the download they answer, while an upload filling a 50 Mbit/s cable upstream beside a 600 Mbit/s download is 8% of it. Where the upstream is under a twentieth of the downstream (1000/35 cable plans), a full upload beside a busy download is as small a share as acknowledgements and nothing in the throughput tells them apart, so that upload's line is found while the download is quiet and kept from then on; until then it climbs back after each cut as one with no line found, which is how every direction climbed before this change. A run with nothing learned starts each direction at 25 Mbit/s. Where the line was found is kept beside the rate in `/var/lib/billet-uplink/learned`, and both are resumed as they were after a restart or a reboot, however long the shaper was down, the rate no higher than nine tenths of the line, so a run stopped while it probed past the line does not start the next one overrunning it; a record from before this, which carries no format, counts as nothing learned, because the shaper that wrote it started each run at its ceiling, recorded that before it had measured anything, and returned a rate toward it for every hour it was down, so a host off for three hours after a cut to 150 recorded 894 of 1000. In the package's model of a 500 Mbit/s line behind a 1 Gbit/s interface, with a download that always wants more and every overrun shown at once (a model, not a measurement), a shaper with no ceiling and nothing learned found the line within two minutes, and over the next half hour overran it in 6.8% of the rounds on a line that drops and 2.3% on one that queues, carrying 94% of it; without the slow climb near the line the same model overran it in 37% and 19% of the rounds. Starting low also gives each reflector's baseline an idle line to measure: in the same model a shaper started at the interface's speed into a line that queues took the queue as each reflector's baseline and never found the line. The ceiling stays a flag and a role variable for a site that wants one; nothing needs it. Not measured: the same on the real line, which needs the node's work moved off it first, and how long a real dropping gateway's overrun takes to show in the reflectors' loss. The reference node keeps its 150 until a release carries this and its journal has shown where the shaper holds the line.

- **The first day on the real line with no ceiling (2026-10-18 to 10-20).** The fleet's inventory dropped the reference node's 150 Mbit/s ceiling once the node ran v0.17.0, and its shaper restarted with no ceiling at 10:46 UTC on 2026-10-18, resuming the rates its v0.17.0 run had recorded under the ceiling (91 up, 150 down), and again onto v0.18.0 at 21:51, resuming 190 and 900; neither restart started low or at the interface's speed. From 21:41 UTC on 10-18 to 00:14 on 10-20 a Mac on the same LAN, on Wi-Fi (3 ms to the gateway), pinged 1.1.1.1 and the gateway once a second while the node's interface counters, CAKE's rates and drops and the shaper's cuts were read once a minute. Of 94,372 pings to 1.1.1.1, 15 went unanswered; the median minute's median was 10.0 ms and the worst minute's 13.4 ms, and a minute's 95th percentile passed 20 ms in 25 of 1,567 minutes (1.6%), 2 of them among the 53 minutes in which the node averaged 100 Mbit/s or more down and 11 while it averaged under 25, some averaging under 1 Mbit/s down (158.7 ms at 04:16 on 10-19, and 97.7 ms at 15:00), when the Mac's pings to the gateway itself reached a 95th percentile of 152.0 and 74.6 ms, which points to delay on the local path, without identifying its cause. The shaper took five cuts. Three found the line: on loss at 22:16:57 with 602 Mbit/s moving down, on loss at 00:53:59 with 412, and on a 97 ms rise in the round trip at 04:55:13 with 596, the line it then held as found; the Mac's 95th percentile reached 29.8 ms in the minute before the second. Two were taken on loss while the direction cut carried a trickle, 19 Mbit/s down at 23:41:50 with two of three probes lost and nothing moving up, and 18 Mbit/s up at 04:32:54 with all three lost while 126 Mbit/s came down, each cutting that direction to about 14 Mbit/s for a loss whose cause the shaper had not established; the rule that blames a direction moving half its own last-minute peak predates the ceiling's removal, and #592 tracks it. After the last cut the download climbed back to the interface's 1,000 Mbit/s by 00:14 on 10-20, through the minute that averaged 407 Mbit/s down, the busiest of the period (the busiest up was 224, at 03:49 on 10-19). A raise from Near of the found line upward is allowed only while the node moves nine tenths of the rate over the two-second window, so the last one, from 992 to 1,000, needed about 893 Mbit/s or more received on the interface over two seconds, with the reflectors' delay rise at or below the shaper's 15 ms threshold. The interface also carries LAN transfers, so not all of that need have crossed the internet line, but it is far more than the 500 Mbit/s these records assumed, and 596, where the 97 ms rise came, is not established as the line's capacity. Not measured: a sustained busy period, since a minute's average hides a burst of seconds and no minute averaged more than 407 down, and the shaper's own five-minute report of where it holds the line (#581), which ships in the release after v0.18.0.

## What was not done, and what the operator can still do

- The gateway's session count was not read (its access code is printed on the device). If it approaches its cap during a full CI run, the fixes are on the operator's side: a firmware that raises the cap, or the node behind a router of its own whose connection table is sized for it, with the gateway in passthrough (which does not by itself stop the gateway tracking every session).
- Fewer connections per job is the durable answer at a shared site: `node.registry_mirrors` (billet's site-local pull-through caches for Docker Hub, GHCR and Quay) were not configured on this deployment, and billet has no Go module or apt cache of its own.
- The laptop's WARP client was ruled out as its own cause: its tunnel rekeys every two minutes as designed, its route-change log holds only the machine's own link-local churn, and its DNS proxy adds 10 to 80 ms per uncached lookup over the router's resolver, which is the cost of Gateway resolution and not a fault.
